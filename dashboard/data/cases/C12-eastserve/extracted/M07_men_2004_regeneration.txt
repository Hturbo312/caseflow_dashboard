Regeneration driven by community web link
THE once proud industrial landscape of east Manchester is home to a project bridging the digital divide and driving forward regeneration.

THE once proud industrial landscape of east Manchester is home to a project bridging the digital divide and driving forward regeneration.
For more than 55 per cent of the community is now linked to the web thanks to a scheme that has put the area at the forefront of wireless broadband access .
Using a successful bid to the government's Wired Up Communities fund, regeneration experts have managed to subsidise a combination of WiFi - or wireless technology - PCs and technical support.
The package has resulted in more than 3,500 homes in the area being equipped with varying levels of Internet access as well as 17 schools and eight online centres.
The majority of people have gone the whole hog and picked up new PCs from east Manchester's New Deal Community for £200 and connected up to the area's always-on WiFi high-speed broadband network for just £16 a month.
Each home needs only a small antenna and a card for their computer to give them access to the network. And for just £6 a month, others have opted for a free set-top box and access to e-mail and the Eastserve.com Intranet which provides news and advice angled at the community.
More than 90 per cent of takers have gone for the new computers thanks to a deal with a local credit union to provide low-cost loans.
Principal regeneration officer Sean McGonigle explained that they were desperate for the people of east Manchester not to be excluded from the benefits that the Internet could offer.
Regeneration
"In terms of regeneration, people need ICT skills and access to ICT otherwise an area is in danger of falling even further behind," he said.
"So, after running a small-scale pilot project to get 350 recycled computers into the community we bid for the Wired Up Communities cash and secured around £1.3m to tackle the initial pilot and to aim to connect 4,500 of the 9,000 homes.
"We wanted to look at innovative ways of including the whole community in the scheme and as there is a low level of cabling in the area - only about 75 per cent of people have landlines - we put the scheme out to tender and ended up with a wireless network."
The project's manager, Bob Jonas, mounted antennae on top of four apartment towers on each corner of east Manchester.
Two of the four receive the Internet through fibre-optic wire.
From there, a radio signal is beamed to 17 schools and eight UK online centres in the area, as well as to other rooftop antennae, which act as distribution points, relaying the signal to individual houses.
Although homes do not have to be in direct line of sight of an antenna to receive the signal, an unobstructed path is helpful.
